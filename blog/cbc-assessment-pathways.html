Understanding CBC Assessment & Pathways: A Plain-Language Guide

25 June 2026 8 min read Ronny Mwenda
RM
Ronny Mwenda Frontend Developer & Mathematics Teacher, Embu County

A recurring conversation with parents and students alike goes something like: "I understand CBC is different from the old 8-4-4 system, but what does that actually mean for assessment, and what on earth is a 'pathway'?" It's a completely fair question — official documentation explains these concepts in policy language that doesn't always translate cleanly into "what does my child actually do, and why."

Here's the plain-language version, written from the classroom and tutoring side rather than the policy side.

What CBC assessment is actually trying to measure

The core shift from the old system isn't just "different exams" — it's a different idea of what's worth measuring in the first place. Rather than relying almost entirely on a single set of final exams at the end of a learning cycle, CBC assessment is built around tracking a learner's developing competencies — actual skills and capabilities — continuously, throughout the learning process, not just at the very end of it.

In practice, this means assessment happens in two broad forms:

  • Formative assessment — ongoing observation and feedback during regular learning, checking understanding as it develops rather than only at the end. This includes classwork, projects, presentations, and teacher observation of how a learner approaches problems.
  • Summative assessment — assessment at the end of a learning period or level, checking what has been achieved overall.
The shift isn't "less exams." It's "exams stop being the only thing that counts."

Why this matters practically for a learner

For a student, this means classwork, participation, and project-based work genuinely matter to their overall academic picture — not as a token "effort grade," but as a real part of how their competency is understood. For a parent, it means a single bad day on one final exam carries less weight than it would have under a system built almost entirely around final exam performance.

It also means consistent engagement over time matters more under CBC than it did before — which is precisely why accessible, ongoing revision resources (rather than only end-of-term cramming materials) genuinely fit how the system is designed to work.

The Senior School Pathways — what they actually mean

This is the part that generates the most questions. At the Senior School level, learners choose a pathway that aligns with their strengths and interests, rather than following one identical track for everyone. The three broad pathways are:

🔬 Science, Technology, Engineering & Mathematics (STEM)
For learners with strength and interest in sciences and mathematics, preparing toward careers and further study in fields like engineering, medicine, computing, and the physical sciences.
🎨 Social Sciences
For learners oriented toward humanities, languages, business, and social studies — preparing toward careers in law, business, education, journalism, and related fields.
🎭 Arts and Sports Science
For learners with strength in creative arts, performing arts, or sports — recognising talent areas that the previous system gave comparatively little structured space to.
// Why this is a genuine shift

Under the previous system, nearly every learner sat the same broad set of subjects regardless of where their strengths actually were. Pathways are a deliberate move toward recognising that a learner strong in art or athletics deserves a structured, respected academic route — not just an after-school activity squeezed in around a fixed academic track that wasn't built with them in mind.

What this means for choosing a pathway

The honest, most useful advice here is to treat the pathway choice as a reflection of demonstrated strength and genuine interest — built from a learner's actual performance and engagement pattern across earlier grades — rather than a decision made from outside pressure about which pathway "sounds more prestigious." A learner genuinely strong in Social Sciences who's pushed into STEM because of family expectation is set up for a harder road than one allowed to follow demonstrated strength.

The common thread with assessment

Pathways and continuous assessment are really the same underlying philosophy expressed two different ways: recognising that learners differ, and that a single rigid track measured by a single high-stakes exam doesn't capture that difference well. Once that connection is clear, both pieces of CBC make a lot more sense as one coherent system, rather than two separate things to memorise for a parents' meeting.
