5 CBC Maths Topics Kenyan Students Struggle With Most — And How to Actually Fix It

24 June 2026 8 min read Ronny Mwenda
RM
Ronny Mwenda Frontend Developer & Mathematics Teacher, Embu County

Every CBC maths classroom has its own rhythm, but certain topics produce the same blank stares year after year, regardless of the specific group of learners in front of you. Having taught and tutored across several of these topics, a pattern becomes obvious: it's rarely that students "aren't good at maths." It's almost always that one specific earlier concept was never solidly anchored, and everything built on top of it wobbles as a result.

Here are five recurring trouble spots, what's actually going wrong beneath the surface, and the kind of approach that tends to genuinely help — not just "practice more," but practice differently.

01 Algebraic Fractions

This is probably the single most common stumbling block across secondary CBC maths. Students who are otherwise comfortable with basic algebra suddenly freeze the moment a fraction with a variable in the denominator appears.

// Typical error

Simplify: (x + 3) / (x² - 9)

A common (incorrect) instinct is to cancel the x terms directly, or to treat x² - 9 as if it doesn't factor at all — missing that it's a difference of squares, (x-3)(x+3), which actually cancels cleanly with the numerator.

The root cause usually isn't the fraction itself — it's shaky factoring underneath it. Most algebraic fraction problems are really factoring problems wearing a fraction's clothing.

What helps

Before touching a single algebraic fraction, spend a full session purely on factoring patterns — difference of squares, common factors, quadratic trinomials — with no fractions involved at all. Once factoring is automatic, algebraic fractions stop feeling like a new topic and start feeling like "factoring, plus one extra step."

02 Probability — Especially Combined Events

Basic probability (a single die, a single card draw) is usually fine. The wall appears with combined events — "and" versus "or," with replacement versus without, independent versus dependent.

The deeper issue is that students often memorise "multiply for AND, add for OR" as a rule without understanding why, which means the rule falls apart the moment a question is phrased slightly differently from the textbook example.

What helps

Tree diagrams, drawn out fully, every time — even for "obvious" cases — until the visual structure of branching outcomes becomes intuitive. Once a student can see why probabilities multiply along a branch and add across branches, the formula stops being something to memorise and becomes something they can rebuild from scratch under exam pressure.

03 Simultaneous Equations (Especially Word Problems)

Solving simultaneous equations once they're already set up — by substitution or elimination — is rarely the problem. The real struggle is the step before that: translating a word problem into two correct equations in the first place.

// Typical confusion

"A bus ticket costs more for adults than children. Three adults and two children pay KES 850. Two adults and four children pay KES 900." Students often correctly identify two unknowns, but struggle to decide which quantities multiply which variable, and the equations come out scrambled.

What helps

A consistent habit of explicitly defining variables in writing first — "let a = adult fare, c = child fare" — before writing a single equation, every single time, even when it feels unnecessary. This single habit eliminates most of the translation errors, because it forces a pause between reading the problem and rushing into algebra.

04 Trigonometric Ratios in Non-Right-Angled Contexts

SOH-CAH-TOA usually lands fine for right-angled triangles. The trouble starts with the Sine Rule and Cosine Rule, particularly knowing which rule applies to a given triangle, and handling the ambiguous case in the Sine Rule where two different triangles can satisfy the same given information.

What helps

A simple decision habit: before reaching for any formula, label what's actually known on the triangle — sides and angles — and ask "do I have a matching side-angle pair?" That single question almost always points directly to whether the Sine Rule or Cosine Rule is appropriate, turning a memorisation problem into a quick diagnostic one.

05 Indices and Logarithms — The Switch Between Forms

Index laws on their own are usually manageable. The real friction appears when a problem requires converting fluidly between index form and logarithmic form, especially in equations like 2^(x+1) = 18, where students aren't sure whether to take logs immediately or simplify first.

What helps

Treating logₐ(b) = c and a^c = b as literally the same sentence, written two different ways — not two separate topics to learn. Once that equivalence feels automatic rather than something to translate consciously, logarithmic equations become far less intimidating.

The common thread

Across all five of these, the same pattern shows up: the visible struggle is almost never the topic itself. It's a gap in a prerequisite skill that the new topic simply exposes. Strong algebra exposes weak factoring. Probability word problems expose a shaky grasp of "and/or" logic. Simultaneous equations expose rushed translation habits. That's actually good news for both students and tutors — it means the fix usually isn't "do more of this topic," but "go back one step and solidify the thing underneath it."
